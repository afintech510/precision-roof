## 1. EXECUTIVE FINDINGS

- **PageSpeed Insights (PSI) is unreachable from this research environment.** Every one of 9 spaced attempts (over ~25 minutes, mixed with unrelated queries to pace requests) against `googleapis.com/pagespeedonline/v5/runPagespeed` returned HTTP 429, for mobile and desktop, on both `liroofingco.com` and no other domain was even attempted after the pattern was clear. This is a hard re-confirmation of the baseline's Session 1 finding — the blocker is not fixed and does not appear pace-able within a WebFetch-only tool environment. **Core Web Vitals numbers in this report are NOT measured.** Anyone claiming otherwise for this project has fabricated data. See §2 and §6.
- Because direct CWV measurement failed, "is anyone actually fast?" is **answered by stack inference, not data**: the field splits cleanly into one custom-built site (LI Roofing Co., on Next.js) and six-to-seven classic WordPress+page-builder sites (Elementor, Jupiter X, Enfold), which is the single most reliable performance-relevant signal this lane could gather without PSI access. WordPress+Elementor stacks are a well-documented performance liability (heavy CSS/JS payloads, render-blocking builder assets) relative to a static/SSG-generated site — this is textbook, not measured-here.
- **Stack detection (measured from page source, sitemap, and generator comments):** WordPress dominates 6 of 8 competitors profiled. Rank Math confirmed via sitemap generator comment on County Roofing Systems and PJ Fitzpatrick (pjfitz.com) — this **confirms** the baseline's claim of Rank Math on at least two sites. Yoast SEO confirmed via sitemap comment on Valor Roofing and Rapid Roofing. Renew Roofing runs the Enfold theme (Kriesi) — visible in a footer credit. Bumble Roofing is WordPress on a subdirectory/multisite path (`wp-content/uploads/sites/50/`). Perfect Pitch runs WordPress on the Jupiter X page builder. **LI Roofing Co. is the outlier**: `/_next/image` URL patterns are unambiguous evidence of a custom Next.js build, no WordPress asset paths anywhere, and its `sitemap.xml` carries no Yoast/Rank Math generator comment — consistent with a programmatically generated (likely `next-sitemap` or a custom route) sitemap rather than a plugin.
- **Platform recommendation: Astro (SSG) + a lightweight headless CMS (Sanity or a Git-based CMS), not WordPress, not Webflow, not a bespoke Next.js app.** Full reasoning and the counterargument defense are in §3 below — this is a defensible middle path between WordPress's editor-friendliness and a custom Next.js app's engineering overhead, and it directly targets the market's actual weakness (slow, plugin-bloated WordPress builds visible in every competitor except one).
- **Programmatic town pages are not the risk — undifferentiated programmatic town pages are.** Google's current spam policy (quoted from developers.google.com, fetched 2026-08-01) defines doorway/scaled-content abuse by *purpose and uniqueness*, not by *method of generation*: pages "would be indistinguishable if the place name were removed" is the operative test. A templated page with genuinely distinct, town-specific data slotted in (permit costs, building-dept contact, housing stock era, named neighborhoods) is not a doorway page under Google's own definition, even if it's produced by a repeatable content pipeline. This directly informs the hybrid model in §4.
- **NY is the single worst state in the country for ADA web-litigation exposure right now.** 1,108 state-court digital-accessibility lawsuits were filed in NY in 2025 alone — 28% of the entire US total, and the #1 state by volume (UsableNet tracker, cited via Forbes/industry trackers, 2026). 64% of sued companies had under $25M revenue. A small Suffolk County contractor site is well within the target profile for the volume-filing law firms driving this (a small number of firms file the large majority of suits). This is a real, quantifiable, non-boilerplate risk for this build — see §7.
- Suffolk County is **22.8% Hispanic/Latino** by population (2024 ACS estimate via Data USA/FRED-sourced figures) — high enough that a Spanish-language site strategy is worth evaluating in a later phase, but not high enough alone to justify hreflang variants at launch given the budget constraint; recommend deferring, not dismissing.
- Dynamic Number Insertion (DNI) for call tracking is **not** a NAP-consistency risk if implemented correctly: the canonical number stays in HTML/schema, only the *displayed* number to a visiting session swaps. This resolves a plausible-sounding but outdated SEO objection to call tracking — multiple independent vendor/industry sources converge on this, though all are vendor-adjacent (see §8 sourcing notes).

## 2. CORE WEB VITALS BENCHMARK — WHAT COULD AND COULD NOT BE MEASURED

**Method attempted:** `GET https://www.googleapis.com/pagespeedonline/v5/runPagespeed?url=<encoded-url>&strategy=mobile|desktop` via WebFetch, targeting homepage + one town page for each of 8 competitors, mobile and desktop (32 planned data points). Requests were paced — spaced out over roughly 25 minutes and interleaved with unrelated WebSearch calls to avoid hammering the endpoint — per the brief's explicit instruction to pace and retry.

This was not a per-request rate limit that pacing could work around; it presents as a shared/exhausted quota on the calling channel itself (the anonymous PSI API quota is small — historically ~400 requests/day and low per-second bursts — and is plausibly shared across concurrent WebFetch tool users on this infrastructure, not scoped to this session alone). Any table populated from memory or estimation would violate the "distinguish measured from claimed" rule this lane is built on. This is a placeholder that must be filled by a follow-up pass using either (a) an authenticated PSI API key (removes the anonymous-quota ceiling), (b) direct Lighthouse/Chrome DevTools tooling in a real browser session (the `web-perf` capability referenced in this environment's own tooling, which was not available to this text-only research lane), or (c) a different network path than this session's WebFetch proxy.

### The recommendation: **Astro (static-first) + a hosted headless CMS (Sanity), not a Git-based CMS, not WordPress, not Webflow.**

Reasoning against the actual constraints in the brief:
1. **Image-heavy + 100+ pages growing from 20–40** is exactly the profile static generation with build-time image optimization was built for. Astro's zero-JS-by-default architecture and island hydration give it a materially higher performance ceiling than any WordPress build in this market, and the entire competitive field's stack choice (WordPress+builder) is the market's visible weak point per §2's inference — beating it on raw speed is achievable without heroics.
2. **Non-technical editing** is solved by Sanity Studio, not by the SSG itself. Sanity's hosted Studio gives a form-based, structured editing experience (not raw Markdown/Git) that a roofing-company owner or office manager can use without understanding deployment — this is the detail that distinguishes this recommendation from a bare Astro+Markdown or Astro+Decap setup, which would fail the "non-technical" requirement in practice even though it's cheaper.
3. **Programmatic scaling to 100+ pages** maps directly onto Sanity's structured content model: a `town` document type and a `service` document type, with Astro's `getStaticPaths` generating the full town×service matrix at build time. This is the same content-modeling discipline that makes the hybrid programmatic/hand-written model in §4 achievable — the structured fields (permit info, housing stock, named neighborhoods) become required, non-optional fields in the CMS schema, which is a real mechanism (not just a policy) for preventing the thin-doorway-page failure mode Valor and Renew exhibit.
4. **Small-business budget**: ongoing infra cost approaches zero (static hosting), and Sanity's free/low tier covers a single-location contractor's editor-count needs. The build cost is front-loaded and higher than a WordPress template purchase, but the 3-year TCO comparison (Astro <$200/yr vs. WordPress $700–2,100/yr, per the industry ranges found) closes much of that gap, and eliminates the recurring plugin-security-patching liability that is a real operational cost of WordPress, just not one that shows up in a hosting invoice.

### Defense against the strongest counterargument

**The strongest counterargument: "This is a small roofing contractor, not a tech company. WordPress is what every agency in this vertical already knows how to build and maintain; a bespoke Astro+Sanity stack means the client is dependent on one developer or shop for any future structural change, and if that relationship ends, they own a stack nobody local can service — WordPress developers are commodity labor, Astro/Sanity developers are not, especially for a Long Island small business ecosystem."**

This is a real risk and should not be waved away. The counter is threefold:
- **The lock-in asymmetry runs the other way for content, not code.** Sanity's content lives in a portable, exportable dataset independent of the front end — the client's actual asset (the town-page content library, the review data, the case studies) is not trapped in a proprietary builder format the way it would be in Webflow, and is arguably *more* portable than a WordPress database entangled with a specific page builder's shortcode/block markup (migrating *off* Elementor or Divi content is its own well-known pain).
- **"Commodity WordPress labor" is precisely why the competitive field looks the way it does.** Every competitor sampled runs some flavor of WordPress+builder, and the market bar (per the baseline's H1 and this lane's stack findings) is visibly low — six of eight competitors don't even have a working financing page, per the baseline. Commodity build quality has not produced commodity results; it has produced a market of interchangeable, mediocre sites. The client is not buying "easy to hand off to any WordPress freelancer" as a strategy; they are buying differentiation, and stack choice is one of the few genuinely defensible technical differentiators available given the content/SEO strategy is otherwise replicable by a competitor with a bigger budget.
- **Mitigate, don't dismiss, the maintainability risk**: specify the build with conventional, well-documented patterns (standard Astro content collections, no exotic custom tooling), commit to thorough handoff documentation, and treat "can a second developer pick this up" as an explicit acceptance criterion in the SOW. This is a process fix, not a reason to default to a worse technical outcome.

**If the client's risk tolerance for developer dependency is genuinely low, WordPress (block editor, minimal plugin surface, a lean custom theme rather than a heavy builder) is the legitimate fallback** — it should not be built with Elementor/Divi given what this lane observed in the competitive set, but a disciplined WordPress build with a hand-coded theme and Rank Math for technical SEO is a real, lower-risk second choice. It is not this lane's top recommendation because it caps the performance ceiling relative to Astro and inherits WordPress's security-patching operational burden, but it is defensible and should be named as the credible alternative in the SOW, not dismissed.

## 4. PROGRAMMATIC VS. HAND-WRITTEN TOWN PAGES — GOOGLE'S CURRENT GUIDANCE AND A HYBRID MODEL

**Google's current position (quoted from developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies, fetched 2026-08-01):**
- Doorway abuse: "sites or pages are created to rank for specific, similar search queries... lead users to intermediate pages that are not as useful as the final destination."
- Scaled content abuse: "many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users," specifically calling out AI generation, scraping-with-transformation, and content-stitching as *methods* that trigger the policy when the *output* is low-value — not because the method itself is disallowed.
- Industry commentary converges (dualmedia.com, upnorthmedia.co, searchfoundry.co.uk — all SEO-agency content, labeled as such, not primary sources) on the operative test being: **"would this page be indistinguishable from another if the place name were removed?"** That phrasing traces back to Google's own doorway-page examples, not to agency speculation.

**The line, stated plainly:** the policy targets *purpose and uniqueness of output*, not *pipeline*. A page generated from a template plus a structured data source is not automatically a doorway page. A page that is genuinely identical to its siblings except for a swapped place name — regardless of whether a human typed it or a script generated it — is.

**Proposed hybrid model:**
1. **Tier the town pages by required uniqueness density, mapped to the content-sourcing protocol Lane D will define.** Every town page gets a mandatory set of *non-swappable* fields sourced per-town (not derivable from a template default): the town building department's permit cost/process (already partially available — the baseline notes Huntington's $400–600 fee via LI Roofing's page), dominant housing-stock era and type, named neighborhoods/hamlets, and at least one roofing-relevant local condition (salt air exposure, tree cover, historic district or HOA prevalence). This is a content-modeling requirement, not an SEO afterthought — it should be enforced as required CMS schema fields (per §3's Sanity recommendation), which makes "just swap the town name" structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged.
2. **Structural pattern (shared, reusable) + content substance (unique, per-town, sourced)** is the same split the baseline already identified empirically: Perfect Pitch's Smithtown page (4 pages total, cited as the best local page found — names Nesconset, Kings Park, Nissequogue, 1960s Capes, Route 347, Smithtown CSD, permitting) is a *hand-written instance of exactly this pattern*, not evidence that hand-writing is required. LI Roofing's 19 pages at 4.5–6.5k words each demonstrate the same discipline at higher volume. Nothing in either example requires abandoning a templated production pipeline — it requires the pipeline to refuse to publish a page until the unique-content fields are filled.
3. **Sequencing implication for launch**: build the CMS schema and the research protocol (Lane D's deliverable) *before* generating any town pages, publish in the order the unique local data is actually gathered (not all 50+ towns simultaneously), and treat "town pages published" as gated by "town research completed," not by template availability. This directly avoids the Valor (48 thin pages) and Renew (24 thin pages) failure mode while still getting the SEO benefit of programmatic *production efficiency* — the efficiency gain is in the pipeline/tooling, not in the published content's uniqueness.

## 5. TECHNICAL SEO REQUIREMENT SPEC

**URL conventions:**
- Service pages: `/services/[service-slug]/` (e.g., `/services/roof-replacement/`) — flat, not nested under a generic `/services/` doorway.
- Town pages: `/[town-slug]/` or `/service-areas/[town-slug]/` — flat is preferable for URL length and matches the pattern of the market's strongest page (Perfect Pitch's Smithtown URL structure was not deep-nested per the baseline).
- Town×service pages (if the matrix from Lane B/D justifies specific combinations): `/[town-slug]/[service-slug]/` — reserve this pattern only for combinations with real, distinct search demand per Lane B's findings; do not generate the full cartesian product.
- Blog/resource content: `/resources/[slug]/` or `/blog/[slug]/`, kept separate from money-page URL space.
- No trailing-slash inconsistency, no mixed case, no session/tracking parameters in canonical URLs.

**Canonical strategy:** self-referencing canonical on every indexable page; canonicalize any filtered/paginated view back to its parent; no canonical chains.

**XML sitemap structure:** split by type (page-sitemap, service-sitemap, town-sitemap, blog-sitemap) rather than one flat file — this mirrors what the stronger competitors in this sample already do (County Roofing and PJ Fitzpatrick's Rank Math output splits into location/service/blog sitemaps) and keeps Search Console indexing diagnostics segmentable by page type, which matters once the town-page count grows past 40–50.

**robots.txt:** disallow any CMS admin/preview paths, allow everything else, reference the sitemap index explicitly.

**Pagination:** for any paginated list (blog index, review list), use `rel=next/prev` equivalents are deprecated by Google but self-referencing canonicals per page plus a "view all" option where feasible remain best practice; avoid infinite scroll without a crawlable paginated fallback.

**Breadcrumbs:** required on every service, town, and town×service page, matched by `BreadcrumbList` structured data (see below), reflecting the actual site hierarchy for internal-linking clarity as much as for the rich-result eligibility.

**Hreflang:** **not recommended at launch.** Suffolk County's Hispanic/Latino population (22.8%, 2024 ACS-based estimate) is meaningful but this figure is ethnicity, not primary-language-at-home — the language-specific figure needed to size this properly was not obtainable in this pass (see §6). Given the small-budget constraint and that zero competitors in the baseline's set were observed running Spanish-language content, this is a phase-2 evaluation, not a launch requirement — flagged, not dismissed.

**404/301 strategy:** every legacy or removed URL gets a 301, not a 404-then-manual-fix; this matters concretely for this project because the baseline documents three competitors (Renew, Bumble, Perfect Pitch) currently running broken financing pages (404/403) — the SOW should explicitly require a redirect map and a recurring (not one-time) broken-link audit, since this is a demonstrated, recurring failure mode in this exact vertical, not a hypothetical risk.

**HTTPS/HSTS:** HTTPS by default (trivial on any modern host); HSTS header with a reasonable max-age once the domain's TLS setup is stable; this is table-stakes and not differentiating, but its absence would be an unforced error.

**Structured data plan (JSON-LD), by page type:**
- Every page (via site-wide layout): `Organization`/`RoofingContractor` (schema.org's `RoofingContractor` type is a valid, specific subtype of `LocalBusiness` — confirmed via schema.org's own type definition) with `sameAs` links, `areaServed` listing every genuinely-serviced town (not an inflated list), `aggregateRating` (only if real, verifiable review counts back it — the baseline notes four of seven competitors show no reviews at all, so under-claiming here is safer than over-claiming).
- Homepage: `WebSite` entity, `LocalBusiness`/`RoofingContractor` primary block.
- Service pages: `Service` schema per page (one service page → one Service block, not a cram-everything-in approach), with `areaServed`, `provider` referencing the Organization, and `offers` only where a real price or price range is published — echoing the baseline's finding that only two of seven competitors currently publish pricing, which is a differentiation opportunity this lane can specify but does not own strategically (Lane D/G).
- Town and town×service pages: `Service` + `FAQPage` (if genuine FAQ content exists — FAQ schema is explicitly valuable for AI-answer-engine extraction per current guidance, independent of its reduced desktop rich-result visibility since 2023) + `BreadcrumbList`.
- Any page with genuine third-party reviews displayed: `Review`/`AggregateRating`, sourced from a real, auditable feed — not self-hosted testimonials styled as reviews (the reference site's `reviewshark.com` pattern documented in the baseline is the anti-pattern to avoid).
- Gallery/before-after content: `ImageObject` with real `caption`/`contentUrl`, town-tagged per Lane D's proposed gallery structure.
- Any video content: `VideoObject` with `thumbnailUrl`, `uploadDate`, `duration`.

**Image optimization:** modern formats (AVIF/WebP with a JPEG fallback), responsive `srcset` generated at build time (native to the Astro recommendation in §3), explicit `width`/`height` attributes on every image to prevent CLS, a defined maximum hero-image weight budget (recommend ≤200KB for above-the-fold hero imagery, ≤100KB for in-gallery thumbnails, enforced at the build pipeline level, not by developer discipline alone).

**Lazy loading:** native `loading="lazy"` on all below-the-fold images; explicitly exclude the LCP hero image from lazy loading (a common, measurable mistake).

**Font loading:** self-host web fonts (avoid a render-blocking third-party font request), `font-display: swap`, limit to 2 font families / a small number of weights to control payload.

**Third-party script budget:** this is where WordPress competitor sites in this market are almost certainly losing performance (inferred, not measured, per §2) — recommend a hard budget of no more than 3–4 third-party scripts at launch (analytics, call tracking, one review widget, one chat/booking tool if adopted per Lane F), all loaded async/deferred, with a named owner responsible for auditing script count quarterly. Every additional third-party tag (heatmap, chat widget, extra pixel) should require an explicit performance-budget tradeoff decision, not silent accumulation — silent accumulation is the visible failure mode across the competitive set's builder-based sites.

## 6. ANALYTICS AND MEASUREMENT PLAN

- **GA4**: property configured with enhanced measurement, plus explicit custom events for the calls that actually matter to this business — `phone_call_click` (via GTM trigger on `tel:` links, since GA4's auto-tracking does not reliably catch this per current guidance), `form_submit` (fired on the form's success callback via dataLayer push, not on thank-you-page pageview, to avoid duplicate counting from back/forward/refresh), and any booking-confirmation event once Lane F's booking layer is specified. Mark all of these as GA4 "key events" (conversions).
- **Search Console**: verified at launch, sitemap submitted (split by type per §5), coverage and Core Web Vitals reports monitored monthly — this is also the only free, ongoing source of real CWV data (via CrUX field data) once the site has enough traffic, which resolves this lane's PSI-access gap for the *client's own site* going forward even though it couldn't be used competitively here.
- **Call tracking**: implement DNI (dynamic number insertion) with the canonical business number preserved in HTML/schema markup and only the *displayed* number swapped per visiting session — this is the mechanism that avoids the NAP-consistency risk that a naive call-tracking implementation could introduce, and it is a solved, well-documented pattern (not something to improvise). Vendor selection is explicitly out of this lane's scope (Lane F owns conversion tooling); this lane specifies the *requirement* (preserve canonical NAP in source/schema) that any vendor choice must satisfy.
- **Heatmapping**: optional, budget-dependent; if adopted, it counts against the third-party script budget in §5 and should be time-boxed (e.g., run for a defined evaluation window rather than permanently loaded).
- **KPI set this build should be judged on**: organic sessions to town/service pages (segmented by page type via the split sitemap structure), phone-click and form-submit key-event counts (not raw sessions — sessions without a captured event are not usable for the business), Search Console average position for the town×service term set Lane B defines, PSI/CrUX field-data Core Web Vitals status (once measurable post-launch), and — the metric competitors in this market conspicuously fail to make visible — a real, verifiable review count/rating displayed consistently across GBP, site schema, and any third-party widget.

## 7. ACCESSIBILITY AND LEGAL EXPOSURE

**WCAG 2.2 AA requirements relevant to this build:** WCAG 2.2 (October 2023) added six new Level A/AA criteria beyond 2.1: Focus Not Obscured (Minimum, 2.4.11 — sticky headers/footers must not fully hide the currently focused element), Dragging Movements (2.5.7 — no drag-only interaction, relevant if any gallery/before-after slider uses drag), Target Size Minimum (2.5.8 — touch/click targets ≥24×24 CSS px, directly relevant to mobile CTA buttons and phone-number tap targets, which is the single most-clicked element on a roofing contractor site), Consistent Help, Redundant Entry (don't force re-entry of already-provided information — relevant to any multi-step estimate form Lane F specifies), and Accessible Authentication Minimum (not relevant unless a login/portal is built). None of these are exotic; all are achievable by a competent build without special tooling, but they must be named explicitly in the spec because they are new enough that a template or theme built before 2023 will not satisfy them by default — worth flagging given how many competitors in this market run older WordPress themes.

**NY ADA litigation exposure — this is real, not boilerplate:**
- 1,108 state-court digital-accessibility lawsuits filed in New York in 2025 — 28% of the entire national total, making NY the #1 state by volume (UsableNet tracker data, cited via multiple 2026 industry sources, consistent across independent citations).
- Nationally, private ADA Title III website suits against small businesses rose 27% in 2025, and 64% of sued companies had annual revenue under $25M — squarely the size profile of a Suffolk County roofing contractor.
- A small number of law firms (cited as roughly 16 firms filing over 90% of suits in H1 2025) drive this as a volume litigation model, not scattered individual complaints — meaning exposure is closer to "a matter of when a scanning bot flags the site" than "an aggrieved individual user complains." This changes the risk calculus from "unlikely" to "should be actively mitigated as a cost of doing business in this state."
- Over 95% of these suits settle out of court within 6–12 months — the practical exposure is a settlement/legal-fee cost, not a trial risk, but it is a real and predictable line item, and demand-letter volume specifically targeting small NY businesses is the documented pattern.
- **Recommendation**: build to WCAG 2.2 AA from day one (cheaper than remediation), commission or run an automated accessibility scan (axe or equivalent) plus one manual keyboard/screen-reader pass before launch, and publish an accessibility statement — none of this is exotic, all of it is standard mitigation, and its absence is a specifically New York risk this lane can confirm is elevated relative to the rest of the country, not a generic disclaimer.

**Caveat on sourcing**: the ADA litigation figures above come from industry accessibility-vendor trackers and blog summaries (UsableNet, and secondary citations of it), not from a primary court-filing database this lane could query directly. UsableNet's tracker is a widely-cited industry-standard source for this specific statistic, but it is also a company that sells accessibility remediation services, so the 1,108/28% figures should be treated as **vendor-published but industry-standard**, not independently verified against PACER or a state court database.
